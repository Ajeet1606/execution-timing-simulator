Understanding Queues

A programming pattern that guarantees every event is processed, but controls the rate of execution. Bursts of activity are lined up and processed sequentially, rather than being dropped or ignored.

How it Works

Unlike Rate Limiting or Throttling which discard excess events, a Queue buffers them. When events arrive faster than they can be processed, they are added to a line (First-In, First-Out). The system continuously works through the line, ensuring every single event is eventually handled.

🛒 The Grocery Checkout Analogy

A cashier takes 1 minute to scan a basket. If a bus arrives and 5 people rush the register at once, the cashier doesn't throw 4 people out of the store (Rate Limit). Instead, they form a line. It will take 5 minutes, but everyone gets served.

Real-World Use Cases

1. Email Delivery Systems

When a company sends a newsletter to 100,000 users, hitting "send" doesn't try to open 100k connections instantly. The emails are queued and sent in batches over several minutes to avoid spam filters.

Guarantee: 100% Delivery Rate

2. Heavy Computation

If 10 users upload 4K videos for transcoding simultaneously, running them all at once would crash the server. A queue processes them one by one, keeping CPU usage stable.

Resource: CPU Protected

3. Ticket Booking (Waiting Rooms)

When concert tickets drop, millions try to buy them. Ticketmaster places users in a virtual queue, ensuring the database isn't overwhelmed while fairly serving users in the order they arrived.

Fairness: First Come, First Serve
